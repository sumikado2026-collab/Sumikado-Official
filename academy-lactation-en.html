Low Milk Supply After Birth: Can Food Increase Breast Milk Production?

If you are worried that you do not have enough breast milk after birth, the first step is not to look for a “milk-boosting” food. No single food can guarantee more milk, and chicken essence is not a treatment for low milk supply. First, check whether milk is being removed effectively and regularly, how well your baby is feeding and growing, and how the mother is recovering.

Direct answer: Breast milk supply is primarily influenced by regular, effective milk removal, together with the health and feeding circumstances of both mother and baby. Drip chicken essence can be part of ordinary postpartum eating, but it should not be presented as a food that increases milk supply or milk quality, or as a replacement for lactation, obstetric, or pediatric care.

First, look at whether the baby is getting milk

A softer breast, the amount from one pumping session, or a crying baby cannot, by themselves, tell you whether supply is enough. A fuller picture is more useful: whether latching is comfortable and effective, whether milk is being removed regularly, and the baby's diapers, alertness, feeding, and growth trend.

  • Effective latch: Position, depth of latch, and sucking can affect how well milk is transferred.
  • Regular milk removal: Breastfeeding or pumping needs to fit the postpartum stage and the baby's feeding needs. A lactation professional can help when you are unsure.
  • The baby's signs: Diapers, feeding, alertness, and growth are usually more useful than a single pumping result.
  • The mother's recovery: Pain, surgical recovery, lack of sleep, emotional strain, and feeling unwell all deserve attention.

You do not have to work it out alone. A brief record of recent feeds or pumping, maternal symptoms, and changes you notice in the baby can make a professional assessment far more useful than trying one food after another.

When is it sensible to ask for an assessment?

What you notice What you can note first Who can help first
Feeding is painful, nipples are damaged, or the baby seems unable to feed well Where the pain occurs, the feeding position, how feeds feel, and what you notice about sucking. A lactation professional or a maternity service that provides breastfeeding support.
You are worried that the baby is not getting enough, is less alert, or is not growing as expected Feeds, diapers, alertness, and the growth trend. Do not rely on crying alone. A pediatric clinician; lactation support can be involved as needed.
Marked breast redness or pain, fever, or feeling acutely unwell When symptoms began, your temperature, any wound, and your overall condition. An obstetrician, family physician, or other medical clinician. Seek prompt care.

Food, soup, and chicken essence belong in everyday nourishment—not as a promise to increase milk supply

After birth, the most useful care is care a mother can sustain: regular meals, drinking when thirsty, opportunities to rest, and practical support from the people around her. These can support overall recovery, but they do not mean that any food directly increases milk supply.

If it fits your routine, Sumikado chicken essence may be taken with a meal or snack on a busy day. It is a food, not a product for increasing milk supply, a treatment for low milk supply, or a substitute for assessing the mother or baby.

Postpartum nourishment starts with caring for both mother and baby

Traditional postpartum practices often remind us to support appetite, rest, and recovery. Modern breastfeeding care looks at latch, milk removal, and infant growth. These perspectives can sit alongside one another, but neither should turn a single food into the answer to a milk-supply concern.

Do not wait for food to solve these postpartum warning signs

Heavy bleeding or blood clots, a severe or persistent headache, chest pain or difficulty breathing, one-sided leg pain or swelling, fever, extreme weakness, or a mental health emergency all need prompt medical attention.

Dr. HANA's perspective

When a mother wonders whether her milk is enough, she often needs more than another product. She needs help making sense of what she and her baby are experiencing. Clarify feeding and recovery first; then make everyday food choices from a steadier, safer place.
